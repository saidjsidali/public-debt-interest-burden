# Economic reasoning journal

For each main result: what was observed, why it might happen, what else could explain it, how
that was checked, what remains uncertain, and what can be concluded.

---

## 1. The typical high-income burden fell while poorer countries' rose

| | |
|---|---|
| **Observed** | Median burden 2015 → 2024: high income 4.0% → 2.3%; low income 3.6% → 8.4%; lower-middle 4.3% → 7.9%. |
| **Mechanism** | Rich governments issued long-dated debt at very low rates before 2022, so the rate shock reaches them slowly; several also earn interest on large asset holdings. Poorer governments borrow shorter, in foreign currency or on market terms, so rate shocks pass through faster. |
| **Alternatives** | (a) Composition: the high-income median is pulled down by net creditors. (b) Measurement: net interest understates cash interest for asset-rich governments. |
| **Checked** | The high-income median implicit rate barely moved (1.8% in 2021 and in 2024) while the low-income one rose from 2.1% to 3.4%. On the gross measure the high-income median was roughly flat (4.8% in 2015, 3.3% in 2021, 4.6% in 2024) rather than falling, so alternative (b) explains part of the net decline. Low-income gross medians rose from 3.7% to 9.1%. |
| **Uncertain** | The maturity structure is not observed directly; the "locked-in" explanation is inferred from slow-moving implicit rates. Only 13 low-income economies have gross data. |
| **Conclusion** | The divergence between rich and poor economies holds on both measures. The *fall* in the rich-country median is partly a feature of the net measure; the report states both. The mechanism is consistent with the evidence rather than proven by it. |

## 2. Debt-to-GDP explains only a quarter of the variation in burdens

| | |
|---|---|
| **Observed** | R² 0.25 in a log-log regression of burden on debt; 0.48 with revenue added. Fifty-two economies are "off-diagonal". |
| **Mechanism** | The identity: burden = rate × debt ÷ revenue. Variation in revenue capacity (8% of GDP in Bangladesh, 51% in France) and in borrowing costs is as large as variation in debt. |
| **Alternatives** | Rich countries might borrow more *because* they borrow cheaply (reverse causality between debt and rates), which would flatten the relationship. |
| **Checked** | Model 4: across countries the implicit rate is unrelated to the debt ratio (coefficient 0.10, interval −0.26 to 0.46), and the debt elasticity of the burden is close to one. No sign of systematic cheaper borrowing at higher debt within the cross-section. |
| **Uncertain** | One-year cross-section; the relationship within countries over time could differ. |
| **Conclusion** | Debt-to-GDP is informative but incomplete; revenue capacity has to be read alongside it. |

## 3. Low revenue is the main reason poor countries' burdens are high

| | |
|---|---|
| **Observed** | Median revenue 16.8% of GDP (low income) against 36.7% (high income), with similar median debt ratios (53% and 57%). |
| **Mechanism** | Narrow tax bases, informality, weak administration, dependence on trade taxes or commodity revenue. |
| **Alternatives** | Higher borrowing costs for poorer countries (debt intolerance). |
| **Checked** | Both channels show up. Holding debt constant, middle-income economies pay about twice the high-income implicit rate. For low-income economies the rate gap is imprecisely estimated, probably because of concessional borrowing. Conditional on debt and revenue, the low-income burden is not significantly different from the high-income one (Table 1, model 3: 0.10 log points, interval −0.39 to 0.59). |
| **Uncertain** | 20 low-income economies is a small sample; intervals are wide. |
| **Conclusion** | For the poorest economies, revenue mobilisation matters most for affordability; for middle-income ones, borrowing costs matter as much. |

## 4. After 2021, rates did the damage

| | |
|---|---|
| **Observed** | Average change 2021–24: +1.0 points = rate +2.0, debt −0.5, revenue −0.6. Rate effect is the largest contribution in 72% of countries. |
| **Mechanism** | Policy-rate increases from 2022 raised the cost of new and refinanced debt. Inflation raised nominal GDP and nominal revenue, lowering debt ratios. |
| **Alternatives** | (a) The implicit rate could rise because of currency depreciation on foreign-currency debt rather than higher rates. (b) Defaults lower measured interest and could mask the rate effect. |
| **Checked** | Medians give the same pattern. The earlier period (2015–21) shows the opposite sign for rates, as the rate cycle predicts. |
| **Uncertain** | The implicit rate mixes interest-rate and exchange-rate effects; the data cannot separate them. |
| **Conclusion** | The rise after 2021 is consistent with the global tightening feeding through to budgets, cushioned by inflation. |

## 5. Falling burdens can be bad news

| | |
|---|---|
| **Observed** | Lebanon 47% → 4%, Ghana 48% (2021) → 25% (2024), Sri Lanka 80% (2023) → 56% (2024). |
| **Mechanism** | Default or restructuring stops or reduces interest payments. |
| **Checked** | Component data: Ghana's interest fell from 7.4% to 3.1% of GDP in 2023, the year after its default; Lebanon's interest fell sharply after its 2020 default. |
| **Conclusion** | A falling burden needs to be read alongside arrears and restructuring events; the ratio alone is not a measure of sustainability. |

## 6. Interest exceeds health spending for 3.7bn people

| | |
|---|---|
| **Observed** | 45 of 177 countries pay more net interest than they spend on health (latest year, mostly 2023). |
| **Mechanism (tempting)** | Interest crowds out health spending. |
| **Alternative** | Health spending in these countries was low before interest costs rose; both may reflect low revenue. |
| **Checked** | Not testable with this design. |
| **Conclusion** | Reported as a descriptive fact about priorities in the budget, not as evidence of crowding out. |
