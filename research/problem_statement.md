# Problem statement

## The economic problem

Interest on public debt is the one item in a government budget that cannot be
cut without a political or legal event. Wages, transfers and investment can be
trimmed; coupons are owed. So the share of revenue that goes to interest is a
direct measure of how much of a government's income is already spoken for
before it decides anything else.

That share rose in many countries after 2021. Two forces pushed it up at the
same time: the stock of debt jumped during the pandemic, and from 2022 central
banks raised policy rates at the fastest pace in four decades. Because
governments refinance their debt gradually, higher market rates feed into the
interest bill with a lag, so the full effect is still arriving.

A rising interest burden matters for three reasons:

1. **Less room for everything else.** Revenue spent on interest is not
   available for health, education or investment. UNCTAD (2024) estimates that
   3.3 billion people live in countries that spend more on interest than on
   either health or education.
2. **Less capacity to absorb shocks.** A government that already devotes a
   fifth of its revenue to interest has less room to borrow in a recession
   without raising doubts about repayment.
3. **A signal used by markets.** Credit-rating agencies treat interest
   payments relative to revenue as a core measure of debt affordability
   (Moody's scores it alongside debt-to-GDP in its fiscal-strength factor), so
   the ratio feeds into borrowing costs.

## Who uses the answer

- Ministries of finance and debt-management offices deciding whether to
  prioritise revenue measures, lengthen debt maturities or slow borrowing.
- International financial institutions running debt-sustainability analysis.
- Sovereign-risk analysts comparing countries that look similar on debt-to-GDP
  but face very different servicing costs.

## Research question

> **How much government revenue is absorbed by interest payments, how has that
> share changed between 2015 and 2024, and is the debt-to-GDP ratio a reliable
> guide to which economies bear the heaviest burden?**

### Secondary questions

1. What share of revenue does the typical government spend on interest, and
   which economies spend the most?
2. How has the burden changed since 2015, and was the change concentrated
   after the 2022 tightening cycle?
3. Is a higher debt-to-GDP ratio associated with a heavier interest burden, and
   how strong is that association?
4. How do burdens differ across income groups, and why?
5. Was the change in burdens driven by higher interest rates, more debt, or
   weaker revenue?
6. What do the patterns imply for fiscal policy and debt management?

## Hypotheses (stated before the main analysis)

| | Hypothesis | Economic reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **H1** | The typical country's interest burden was higher in 2024 than in 2015, with most of the increase after 2021. | Pandemic borrowing raised debt stocks; the 2022-23 rate rises raised the cost of new and refinanced debt. |
| **H2** | Debt-to-GDP is positively but only weakly associated with interest burdens across countries. | The burden is the product of three terms (rate × debt ÷ revenue). Debt is only one of them, and countries able to carry more debt tend to borrow more cheaply. |
| **H3** | At comparable debt ratios, lower-income economies carry heavier burdens because they pay higher interest rates and collect less revenue. | "Debt intolerance" (Reinhart, Rogoff and Savastano, 2003), reliance on foreign-currency and concessional-to-market borrowing, and narrow tax bases. |
| **H4** | Between 2021 and 2024 the rise in burdens came mainly from higher implicit interest rates, partly offset by falling debt ratios as inflation raised nominal GDP. | Market rates rose sharply while high inflation eroded the real value of existing debt. |

These are hypotheses to test, not conclusions. Each one can be rejected by
the data, and the report says so where it is.
